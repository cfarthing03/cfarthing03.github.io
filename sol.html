The Continent of Sin

Sol

The Sleeping City

A nocturnal city recovering from annihilation while Sloth's old culture and new dwarven technology grow together.

At the Table: Arriving in Sol

If your players arrive during the day, Sol can feel almost abandoned. Streets that should be crowded are quiet. Doors are shut. Curtains are drawn. Market stalls sit covered. The only sounds may be the occasional worker finishing a late shift, the hum of machinery from a dwarven district, or wind passing through damaged stone that has not yet been rebuilt. For a few minutes, it is easy to wonder whether the city is still inhabited at all.

Then evening comes. Windows begin to glow. Doors open. Food carts roll into the streets. Music starts somewhere in the distance and is answered by another group several blocks away. Children appear first, then families, workers, merchants, performers, and crowds. Within an hour the city is transformed. Streets that looked dead under the sun are packed under moonlight.

Sol does not feel like a city that sleeps too much. It feels like a city that chose a different clock than everyone else.

The Sleeping City

Sol's inverted rhythm is not a novelty. It is the foundation of daily life. Most residents sleep through a large portion of the day and conduct work, trade, celebrations, worship, and social life at night. Shops are designed around moonlit business hours. Public lighting is extensive. Entire districts are built to become brighter and more active as the sky darkens.

This rhythm is closely tied to Sloth. Rest is not treated as something shameful or lazy. It is treated as a biological and spiritual necessity. People are expected to recover. Taking time to sleep, heal, or step away from work is culturally normal in a way that can surprise visitors from cities where exhaustion is treated like proof of commitment.

That does not mean Sol is slow. At night it can be one of the liveliest cities on the continent.

The Fragment of Greed

Sol's greatest catastrophe did not come from Pride. It came from Greed himself, or more precisely, from a fragment of Greed's body that refused to return.

Greed's immortality works through a cyclical reconstruction. His body eventually separates into pieces, and over time those pieces are drawn back together until he becomes whole again. One fragment did not want that. It had been part of Greed's body, but it developed a desire to remain separate, to continue existing independently, and to be free from the force pulling it back into him.

The fragment grew into an enormous creature and tore through Sol. It was not merely some beast carrying Greed's influence. It was Greed's body acting as something separate from Greed. That distinction is what makes the event so disturbing.

The Destruction

The creature devastated entire sections of the city. Buildings collapsed, streets were torn apart, and neighborhoods vanished beneath destruction that moved faster than many residents could evacuate. The attack was large enough that Sol could not contain it alone.

The Pantheon ultimately defeated the fragment and petrified it. The remains became part monument, part warning, and part wound. Sol did not simply lose buildings. It lost familiar streets, families, traditions tied to specific places, and the sense that the city would always be there tomorrow.

That history now sits underneath almost every conversation about rebuilding.

Living Among Ruins

Modern Sol is a city where repaired buildings stand beside empty foundations. Some neighborhoods are fully alive again. Others remain partially abandoned. A family may live in a newly rebuilt home while looking across the street at the remains of the house where their grandparents lived.

The city does not hide the damage. In many places, the old destruction has simply become part of the landscape. New walkways curve around ruins. Markets use surviving walls as boundaries. Memorials appear in unexpected places because the event was too large to be contained in one official monument.

This gives Sol a strange emotional atmosphere. It is cheerful without pretending nothing happened. Its festivals can be loud, joyful, and crowded while taking place beside visible scars.

The Dwarven Districts

Dwarves moved into portions of Sol during the rebuilding period and brought technology that radically changed those districts. Their neighborhoods are louder, brighter, and more mechanical than the older parts of the city. Metal walkways, mechanical lifts, engineered lighting, reinforced structures, workshops, and unfamiliar machines can make crossing from one district into another feel like crossing into a different era.

The dwarven technology rivals what is found in Oro, and in some fields surpasses it. This has created both cooperation and tension. Some residents see the dwarves as essential partners in rebuilding. Others worry that too much of old Sol will disappear beneath new machinery.

So far, the city has refused to choose one identity. Sol is becoming a mixture of old nocturnal traditions and new technological ambition.

Life After Midnight

To truly understand Sol, a visitor has to stay awake long enough. Midnight is not the end of the evening. It is the middle of the day. Families eat together. Merchants argue over prices. Couples walk beneath lanterns. Musicians perform in public squares. Children play in streets that were silent hours earlier.

There is a softness to Sol's culture that survives even after catastrophe. People take breaks. They sit with one another. They understand that recovery is not instantaneous and that surviving something does not require pretending it did not hurt.

Sol is rebuilding slowly, and almost proudly so. The city has no interest in rushing simply to prove that it can.
