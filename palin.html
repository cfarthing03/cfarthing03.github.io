The Continent of Sin

Palin

City of Warriors

Wrath's desert city, where combat is worship, civic ritual, justice, and sometimes the price of citizenship.

At the Table: Arriving in Palin

There is no skyline.

From the desert ridge, Palin spreads beneath you like an enormous camp that never ended. Thousands upon thousands of collapsible homes cover the sand, some no larger than modest family dwellings, others vast enough to resemble fabric palaces. Their roofs rise in layered peaks and sweeping curves, held by heavy timber frames, reinforced poles, braided cables, and painted supports. Red, ochre, white, black, and sun-faded gold repeat across the city in banners, dyed hides, stitched canvas, painted symbols, family crests, and long strips of cloth snapping in the desert wind.

The structures move even when the people do not. Canvas breathes in and out with every gust. Guy lines hum. Loose tassels twitch. Sand slips around wooden stakes and gathers in thin crescent ridges on their leeward sides. A child runs past one support, catches a foot on the rope, recovers without stopping, and disappears between two homes while an adult yells something after them that the wind tears apart before it reaches you.

Nothing is quite where a city tells you it should be.

A path of packed sand widens into a market, narrows between two family compounds, then simply vanishes because someone has begun dismantling a house directly across it. Three people are pulling enormous support poles from the ground while another folds a wall panel with the practiced speed of someone making a bed. Furniture waits in stacks nearby. A table has been broken down into flat pieces. Cushions are tied into bundles. Clay lamps sit carefully packed in straw-lined crates. A painted door, frame and all, leans against a cart because even the door is meant to travel.

Farther in, a family is erecting another home from nothing. First the poles. Then the central supports. Then layered fabric, wall partitions, rolled flooring, storage racks, and decoration. By the time you pass, a structure that did not exist when you first saw it already has a cookfire burning outside.

The ground beneath your feet is uncertain. Sand gives way to compacted gravel, then to loose stone dumped recently to stabilize a path. Your boots shift half an inch with every step until you learn to shorten your stride. The locals do not. They move quickly across it, bodies adjusting without thought.

Then a shout erupts from somewhere to your right.

Heads turn. A circle opens in the crowd with startling speed. Two people are already fighting.

No one screams. No one runs for guards. Someone carrying a bowl of food steps aside, keeps eating, and leans against a post to watch. A merchant calmly lifts a display of clay cups off a table before one of the fighters stumbles too close. Three children climb onto a cart for a better view. One punch sends a man into the side of a nearby home, and the wall flexes inward rather than breaking. The owner looks up, swears, and starts loosening one of the outer lines before the fight can reach it again.

Beyond all of this, one structure refuses to move.

The Colosseum rises from the center of Palin like a mountain made by hands. Stone terraces catch the sunlight. Massive arches open into shadow. Banners hang from the upper levels, visible above every roof and movable wall in the city. No matter where the neighborhoods drift, the Colosseum remains exactly where it has always been.

Everything else in Palin can move.

The city itself has learned to survive by refusing to stay still.

A City Designed to Move

Palin's homes are collapsible because permanence is inconvenient here. The desert does not always provide food, water, or sanitation in the same places forever. Families move as resources shift. Neighborhoods relocate when waste becomes a problem. Communities reorganize around changing access to water.

Then there are the brawls.

Fighting is common enough that permanent construction would be a constant maintenance nightmare. A large fight can spill through a neighborhood and destroy walls, furniture, supports, or entire homes. A collapsible structure can be moved before a problem reaches it, repaired more easily afterward, or replaced without rebuilding a stone house from the ground up.

Sometimes there is no practical reason at all. People move because they are tired of their neighbors. They move because they want a better view. They move because a friend relocated across the city. They move because they woke up and decided the other side of Palin sounded more interesting.

The result is a city whose geography is constantly changing. A tavern that was beside your house last month may be several streets away now. Directions are often given using people, landmarks, banners, or the Colosseum instead of fixed addresses.

The City of Warriors

Palin is built around Wrath, but Wrath here does not mean uncontrolled anger. Combat is treated as a skill, a social language, a religious practice, a form of entertainment, a test of courage, and sometimes a perfectly acceptable way to settle a disagreement.

Visitors often misunderstand the city's constant fighting. A brawl does not automatically mean people hate one another. Friends fight. Family members fight. Neighbors fight. Strangers fight. Sometimes people fight because they are angry, sometimes because they are bored, and sometimes because they simply enjoy it.

The important distinction is control. Someone who cannot stop when a fight is over is not admired. Someone who attacks a helpless opponent is not impressive. Strength without judgment is considered dangerous rather than honorable.

The Colosseum

The Colosseum is Palin's only major permanent structure, and it dominates the entire city physically and culturally. It is visible from almost anywhere, making it one of the few reliable landmarks in a place where entire neighborhoods can move.

Inside, the Colosseum serves dozens of purposes. Formal combat takes place there. Citizenship is earned there. Religious ceremonies are held there. Major disputes are resolved there. Public celebrations, demonstrations, challenges, tournaments, and moments of civic importance all pass through its arena.

The structure is built to survive a culture that regularly tests the limits of violence. Its walls are reinforced, its interior spaces are designed for enormous crowds, and its staff are trained to intervene when combat approaches the point of death.

Citizenship Must Be Earned

There is only one path to citizenship in Palin. You fight for it.

Anyone who wants to become a citizen must enter the Colosseum and prove themselves through combat. There is no alternate bureaucratic route, no property requirement that bypasses the arena, and no amount of money that substitutes for the test.

The challenge is not simply about defeating another person through brute force. Palin values courage, endurance, judgment, discipline, and the ability to continue when a person would rather quit. A fighter who demonstrates those qualities earns the right to call Palin home.

For residents, this gives citizenship unusual emotional weight. Everyone who holds it knows what it cost.

Wrath's Blessing

Wrath's blessing changes the practical reality of living in the desert. Those who fight in his name within Palin can survive without normal food or water. The blessing is tied to the city and to combat carried out in Wrath's name. Leave Palin and the effect disappears. Return and it can be restored.

This miracle is one of the reasons a city this large can survive in such a hostile environment. Food and water still matter culturally, economically, and socially, but survival is not entirely dependent on them for those receiving the blessing.

It also means combat is literally woven into survival. Fighting is not just something Palin does. It is part of why Palin can exist.

Daily Life in a Moving City

Palin can feel chaotic to outsiders because nothing stays where they expect it to. A neighborhood may wake up to discover half its homes are being packed away. Markets shift with population. Training grounds appear wherever enough open space exists. A street can disappear because three families decided to relocate and another dozen moved into the gap.

Residents are used to it. Homes are designed around portability. Furniture folds, locks, stacks, or breaks down. Decorations are built to travel. Important possessions are stored with movement in mind. Even wealthy households think about mobility in a way that would seem absurd in a stone city.

Palin's people do not see instability. They see freedom. If something breaks, rebuild it. If a place stops working, move. If someone challenges you, fight. Then get on with your day.
