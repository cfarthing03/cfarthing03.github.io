Religion of Charity

The Faith of Charity

The organized worship of Charity, built around generosity, sacrifice, giving, aid.

LIVING FAITH

What worship looks like beyond doctrine

PRACTICE

Open tables, mutual-aid stores, shelter, healing, debt-free gifts, and practical service.

OFFERINGS & RITES

Food, coin, clothing, medicine, tools, and time—especially when the gift is meaningful relative to what the giver has.

COMMUNITY

Temples are judged by what moves back out into the community; a growing treasury beside hungry neighbors is a theological failure.

THE DEBATE

The great argument: where is the line between generous self-sacrifice and teaching others that a giver has no right to keep anything?

Core Teaching

Followers treat giving as a sacred transfer of responsibility: if you possess enough to help, the decision not to help must be justified.

Ordinary Worship

The faith does not require every follower to become clergy. Common devotion is expressed through offerings, mutual aid, shelters, gift rites. The act matters most when it reflects deliberate commitment rather than public performance.

Temples and Clergy

Temples of Charity use the same visual language seen in the god's iconography: gifts, keys, scales, ribbons, reliquaries. Clergy are expected to embody the virtue publicly, which makes failures of doctrine especially politically and spiritually significant.

Miracles and Divine Favor

Miracles attributed to Charity tend to be interpreted through the virtue itself. A believer does not merely ask for power; they ask for the ability to become more completely aligned with Charity's ideal.

The Dangerous Edge

Its harshest sects can turn generosity into compulsory self-sacrifice and use previous gifts as leverage over those who received them.

Open Tables

A common temple practice is the open table: food is served without requiring proof of need, conversion, or repayment. Wealthier congregations may maintain permanent kitchens and stores; poorer communities may simply add one extra portion to every shared meal.

Offerings and Redistribution

Coin placed before Charity is rarely meant to remain ornamental. Temples are expected to move offerings back into the community through food, shelter, healing, education, burial costs, or emergency aid. A treasury that grows while neighbors starve is considered a theological failure.

The Problem of Martyrdom

Charity's ugliest disputes concern how much a good person should be expected to give. Some traditions romanticize total self-sacrifice. Others argue that demanding sacrifice from someone else is the opposite of charity: the giver must remain a person with needs, not become a resource everyone feels entitled to consume.
