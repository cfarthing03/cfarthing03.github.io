The Continent of Sin

Hus

The One Stop Dinner

The continent's largest population center: culinary paradise above, crime capital below.

At the Table: Arriving in Hus

The air changes first.

Before the streets of Hus come fully into view, smoke, spice, yeast, roasted meat, citrus, hot oil, fish, sugar, herbs, fermented grain, and something sharply medicinal layer over one another until separating them becomes impossible. Every few steps one smell dominates, then is replaced by another. Fresh bread gives way to garlic. Garlic disappears beneath charcoal smoke. Charcoal becomes cinnamon. Cinnamon becomes frying onions.

Then the crowd closes around you.

Hus does not seem to have empty space so much as temporary gaps between people. Vendors occupy corners that barely qualify as corners. Carts squeeze through streets already packed with pedestrians. Balconies extend over alleys. Laundry hangs above market stalls. Homes sit over restaurants, workshops sit beneath homes, and staircases climb the outside of buildings because there was nowhere left to put them inside.

The ground is uneven from constant use. Grease-darkened stones near food stalls become slick beneath your boots. Wooden planks bridge shallow gutters where water carries vegetable scraps and soap foam toward covered drains. Crushed herbs cling to the edges of the street. A single green leaf from a bundle of coriander sticks to someone's shoe, travels half a block, then disappears beneath a cart wheel.

Everyone seems to be carrying food.

A child walks past with a skewer too large for both hands. A laborer eats from a bowl while balancing a crate on one shoulder. Two merchants negotiate beside a steaming pot and pause only long enough for the vendor to refill their cups. An elderly woman tears bread in half without looking and gives one piece to a man seated beside her.

The noise is relentless. Knives strike cutting boards. Pans hiss. Vendors shout prices. Customers shout back. Someone laughs loud enough to turn heads three stalls away. Plates clatter. A butcher's cleaver lands with a wet crack. A bell rings from a bakery and a line forms almost instantly.

There is order inside the chaos, but it takes time to see it.

Porters know which side of the street to keep clear. Vendors know exactly how far their stalls may extend before a neighbor complains. Restaurant workers step around one another in movements practiced over years. A delivery cart arrives at a market and six people begin unloading it before the driver has finished climbing down.

Somewhere beneath all of that abundance, the city's darker economy moves just as efficiently.

A man carrying a sealed crate exchanges a glance with a woman at a fruit stall and continues walking. A narrow staircase behind a spice shop has two guards but no sign. Someone presses a small red vial into a sleeve before turning into the crowd. Nothing stops. Nobody stares.

Hus is hungry enough that almost anything can disappear inside it.

The One Stop Dinner

Food is the center of Hus's identity. It is not merely something residents consume. It is how families celebrate, how businesses impress clients, how communities organize festivals, how religions express devotion, and how strangers become welcome.

Meals can carry enormous social weight. A family recipe may be treated like inheritance. A chef can become a local celebrity. Restaurants build reputations that last generations. Neighborhoods are known for particular dishes, ingredients, or cooking styles.

For visitors, Hus offers almost anything they can imagine and several things they probably cannot.

The Church of Gluttony

The Church of Gluttony has historically influenced far more than worship. It has been deeply involved in food quality, ingredient supply, distribution, preparation standards, public meals, and the idea that feeding people well is a sacred responsibility.

Cooking is treated as craft and devotion. A meal can be an offering. Feeding someone who is hungry can be religious service. Preserving a recipe can be an act of cultural memory.

When Church leadership failed, the consequences reached far beyond temples. Food distribution suffered, supplies became unstable, and shortages spread. The Pantheon and surviving Church leadership eventually intervened, but the crisis proved how dependent Hus had become on systems most residents barely noticed while they worked.

A City of Appetite

Gluttony's influence in Hus is broader than food. The city celebrates appetite itself. Hunger for experience. Hunger for comfort. Hunger for wealth. Hunger for pleasure. Hunger for novelty. Hunger for connection.

This gives Hus a culture that rarely tells people to want less. Instead, it asks whether what they are consuming is actually satisfying them.

That distinction matters because excess is easy here. A person can surround themselves with abundance and still feel empty. Hus understands that better than almost anywhere.

The Markets

Hus's markets are enormous and chaotic. Fresh produce sits beside preserved food from distant regions. Fishmongers shout prices over spice merchants. Butchers display cuts unfamiliar to outsiders. Bakers pull trays from ovens while customers line the street.

Some markets specialize in luxury ingredients. Others are brutally practical. There are places where the wealthy buy rare delicacies and places where families stretch a handful of coins into enough food for a week.

The scale of the city means supply never truly stops moving. Carts arrive constantly. Warehouses empty and refill. Entire fortunes depend on keeping food moving through the city before it spoils.

The Crime Capital

Hus is also the crime and drug capital of the continent. That reputation is not separate from its size. The city has the most people, the most goods, the most markets, and therefore the most places for criminal networks to hide.

Smuggling routes run through legitimate supply chains. Stolen goods disappear into enormous markets. Gangs control particular streets or businesses. Life's Blood found a powerful market here because there were always people willing to buy, sell, move, or hide it.

Some districts are safe because communities protect one another fiercely. Others are dangerous because everyone knows exactly who controls them and nobody wants to be the person who tests that control.

Living in Hus

Hus is exhausting and comforting at the same time. Privacy is difficult. Silence is rare. Someone is almost always nearby. For residents who love crowds, food, family, and constant social contact, there may be nowhere better.

The city's density creates strong neighborhood identities. People know which cook makes the best breakfast, which alley to avoid after dark, which shopkeeper will extend credit, which gang controls the next district, and which restaurant is worth waiting three hours for.

Hus is a city that constantly asks what you are hungry for. The answer can be dinner. It can also be something much more dangerous.
