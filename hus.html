The Continent of Sin

Hus

The One Stop Dinner

The continent's largest population center: culinary paradise above, crime capital below.

At the Table: Arriving in Hus

Your players smell Hus before they understand its size. Smoke, bread, spices, roasting meat, frying oil, sweet pastries, fermented drinks, herbs, and dozens of unfamiliar foods mix together in the air. The streets are crowded, noisy, and alive at almost every hour.

Hus feels packed because it is. It is the most densely populated city on the continent, and nearly every available space has been turned into something useful.

The One Stop Dinner

Food is not a side feature of Hus. It is the center of civic identity. Meals are business meetings, religious ceremonies, family traditions, celebrations, political tools, and public entertainment.

The city's best chefs are known far beyond Hus, and the constant movement of people and goods gives them access to ingredients from across the continent.

The Church of Gluttony

The Church of Gluttony has historically played a major role in the city's food supply. Ingredient quality, preparation, distribution, and public meals all carry religious importance because feeding people is treated as an act worthy of attention and skill.

The Church has suffered periods of instability, including leadership failures that disrupted food distribution and pushed parts of the city toward shortage. The Pantheon and surviving Church leadership eventually intervened.

A City That Is Always Hungry

Gluttony's influence in Hus is not limited to eating. The city celebrates appetite in a broader sense: appetite for experience, comfort, wealth, novelty, community, and pleasure.

The Crime Capital

Hus is also widely known as the crime and drug capital of the continent. Its enormous population and constant flow of goods make it ideal for smuggling, organized crime, theft, black markets, and substances such as Life's Blood.

That does not make every district dangerous. Some neighborhoods are warm, communal, and fiercely protective of their residents. Others are controlled by gangs, smugglers, or people who know exactly which parts of the food trade are easiest to exploit.
