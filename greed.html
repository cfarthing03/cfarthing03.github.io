The Seven Sins

Greed

Ambition, ownership, prosperity, exchange—and the promise that even death can be negotiated with.

Greed is one of the rare Sins whose story begins completely mortal. His divinity grew out of contracts, ambition, and an absolute refusal to surrender what he had built.

The Mortal Who Became Greed

Greed began as a mortal man with an extraordinary talent for contracts. He used that talent to build a kingdom from nothing, turning agreements, obligations, and carefully chosen words into an empire powerful enough to outlive most of the people who helped create it.

Eventually he found the one problem wealth could not solve: death. Greed was terrified not simply of dying, but of losing everything he had made. His kingdom, his influence, his power, and his identity were all things he considered his. Giving them up because his body stopped working was, to him, an unacceptable clause in the contract.

Greed's Everlasting Contract

Rather than accept mortality, Greed divided himself into five fragments. Each fragment carried a portion of his power and one of the abilities he had possessed while whole. Through a contract with Sloth, Greed arranged to be reborn every thousand years. Each new incarnation is naturally drawn toward those fragments, and recovering them gradually restores the being that Greed once was.

It is perhaps the most Greed solution imaginable: a man wanted to keep what was his so badly that he made even death negotiate.

Contract Magic

Greed is the source of Contract Magic, one of the defining forces of Hilnem. A magical contract can enforce the terms willingly accepted by its participants and apply whatever consequences were written into the agreement. Some people possess a natural affinity for creating these contracts; others rely on specialists, businesses, and families such as the Vales.

The magic enforces an agreement. It does not make that agreement fair. A badly written contract can be harsh, exploitative, full of loopholes, or simply disastrous for someone who did not bother to read it. In Hilnem, precision is not just professionalism. It can be self-preservation.

What Greed Means Now

Modern worship does not treat wealth or desire as inherently shameful. Greed represents ambition, the right to own what you have earned, the value of exchange, and the responsibility created when one person promises something to another. Wanting more can build a business, improve a family, or create an entire city. The question is what you are willing to trade to get it.

Hilnem reflects that philosophy. Its contracts make it one of the safest places on the continent, sometimes to absurd degrees. There are buildings where simply walking through the door means agreeing not to commit a crime inside. Greed's city trusts rules because, unlike people, the rules can be made impossible to ignore.
