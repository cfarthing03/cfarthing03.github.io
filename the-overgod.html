The Overgod

Some truths about the gods are dangerous. Others are dangerous because they reveal that the gods are not the highest thing in the room.

What an Overgod Is

Gods rule pieces of existence. A city. A hunger. A virtue. A sin. An idea made powerful enough to answer when someone calls its name. An Overgod sits above that arrangement. It does not simply rule one piece of a universe. It bears responsibility for the universe itself.

The closest description the archive can preserve is that the universe resides within the Overgod's chest. Attempts to explain that literally fail almost immediately. It is not a hollow cavity containing stars. It is not a portal. It is not a miniature copy of creation. It is the point at which the universe and the being responsible for it become impossible to separate cleanly. To wound one at that scale is to disturb the other.

The Overgod created this world. Its laws, its boundaries, the space in which its gods could exist, and the conditions under which life could continue were all his work. That is why his involvement cannot be understood as a ruler interfering with a kingdom. He was interfering with something he believed he had made and was therefore obligated to correct.

The Strings

The word Strings is useful because almost every other word is worse.

Those capable of perceiving them describe reality as threaded through with lines that were not there a moment earlier and may not remain once noticed. They can appear between a person and the place they stand, between an object and the fact that it has weight, between an event and the memory that it ever occurred. Some seem to stretch across impossible distances. Others terminate in nothing. Two observers looking at the same thing may not see the same String, even when both are powerful enough to perceive them.

A String can be grasped. That does not mean a hand closes around matter. It means the user has found a way to make intention behave as though it were something tactile. Pulling one may change a relationship. Twisting one may alter how a thing is allowed to exist. Breaking one may remove the condition that permitted it to exist at all. The gesture is real. The resistance is real. The consequence is real. The String itself is not, at least not in the way a blade or a bone is real.

What the archive believes: Strings are a visualization produced when a mind with sufficient authority attempts to take responsibility for some part of reality and change it. Reality presents that responsibility in a form the user can manipulate. The String is therefore not the power. It is the shape the power takes when the user needs something to hold.

This is why ordinary people cannot see them. There is nothing hidden from their eyes. There is no invisible web waiting for better vision. Until someone possesses the capacity to alter the thing in question, there may be no String there at all. Then, in the instant reality must be changed, there is.

That explanation creates its own problems. If a String only exists when required, then what exactly is being grasped? If two users perceive different Strings, which one corresponds to the true structure of the world? If a String can be shattered, what is actually breaking? The answer appears to be that all of these questions assume reality has a single underlying shape waiting to be discovered.

No. Still too literal.

The Creator of This World

The Overgod's history is not one intervention. It is a sequence of attempts to solve the same problem with increasingly severe answers.

Across the three major arcs, his conclusion changes. First, he believes corruption can be contained. Then he decides humanity itself is the source. Finally, he abandons correction entirely and prepares to erase the universe. Each step follows from the failure of the one before it.

First Intervention

Tales of the Old Six

The earliest plan was containment.

The Overgod looked at his creation and identified qualities he considered intolerable. Cruelty. Hunger. Violence. Pride. Desire. Everything he believed made the rest of creation uglier simply by existing beside it. Rather than remove those qualities from the universe entirely, he attempted to confine them. If the worst parts of the world could be gathered into one place, the rest could remain beautiful.

The system was not merciful to those trapped inside it. That was not its purpose. Its purpose was isolation. The cycle existed so that suffering, corruption, and everything the Overgod rejected could be concentrated where it would not spread.

The Old Six broke that cycle.

From their perspective, they escaped a system that had no right to own them. From his, they tore open a containment structure built to protect everything outside it. The important part is not which description is kinder. The important part is that the first solution failed, and the Overgod did not forget why.

The first response after the cycle was broken is not available in this record.

Second Intervention

Clockwork Resurgence

By Clockwork, the Overgod had stopped treating humanity's worst qualities as something that could be separated from humanity itself.

He had watched mortals break the system meant to contain them, rebuild, reproduce the same cruelties elsewhere, and then defend their right to continue doing so. His conclusion was simple and catastrophic: the problem was not the sins. The problem was the species capable of choosing them.

Pride became the instrument again. Through Pride, an army of machines could replace what the Overgod no longer trusted. Machines did not need to inherit the same instincts, fears, hungers, or appetites. Humanity could be removed, the world could remain, and creation could begin again without the element he now considered irreparable.

The people of the world resisted. They did not regard themselves as a defect in someone else's design. They fought the army, fought Pride, and survived the second correction.

Final Intervention

The Overgod's Revenge

The third plan abandoned preservation.

The Horsemen were a contingency for a universe the Overgod no longer intended to repair. Their purpose was not to rule what remained. They were meant to end it. Once summoned, the existing universe would be deleted and the Overgod would begin again from nothing.

Even then, he did not choose indiscriminate annihilation. Seven mortals were selected to survive the replacement. He chose people who had experienced humanity's horrors firsthand, people who could understand the argument he was making because they had lived inside the evidence for it. Each was given a special gift. In return, they were expected to help bring the old world to its end.

They were called The Virtues. At this point they were not gods. They were seven chosen mortals, each offered a place in the world that would come after this one.

The Virtues succeeded in summoning the Four Horsemen.

The party stopped them.

And for the first time, the conflict did not end with another failed instrument or another plan interrupted at a distance. The party reached the Overgod himself.

The account of the confrontation is incomplete.

The Death That Wasn't

History remembers the result as the death of the Overgod because there is no ordinary word for what happened.

They did not destroy the being itself. They severed his connection to their reality.

An Overgod is not simply a creature living inside the universe it governs. The relationship runs in both directions. The universe depends on that governing presence, and the Overgod's authority is expressed through the universe attached to it. Cutting him away therefore did two things at once: it removed his ability to continue controlling this reality, and it left the reality without the thing that had been carrying the responsibility for its continued existence.

That should have been a disaster without a solution.

It was not an accident.

Recovered interpretation

By the end of the third conflict, the Overgod understood that he could not force this world back into obedience. Every solution produced another rebellion. Every correction required more destruction than the one before it. If he remained, the struggle would continue until either he erased the world or the world found a way to remove him.

He chose the second outcome, but on terms that guaranteed his removal would carry a cost.

The confrontation gave the party a way to sever him from reality. Doing so freed the world from his control while simultaneously creating a problem only his successors could solve. The victory was real. So was the trap hidden inside it.

Odin's Choice

The severance left the universe without an Overgod. That vacancy could not remain empty indefinitely. Someone had to take responsibility for the reality the party had just freed, or the structure they saved would eventually fail.

Odin could not simply take the position himself.

He was an illborn Overgod. He had not been born as one. He had acquired the power artificially. That made him capable of wielding Overgod abilities, including powers close enough to the Strings to be mistaken for true authority, but it did not make him a valid replacement for the being the universe had lost.

Two members of his family could become what the world now required.

His daughter was a half-born Overgod, mortal and Overgod from birth. His wife was mortal, but her Chronomancy gave her a path to survive the transformation that should have killed her.

Odin had to choose which one of them would pay for everyone else continuing to exist.

He chose his wife.

She remains alive because Chronomancy continually refuses to let the process finish killing her. Her body is torn apart by the responsibility placed inside it and restored before death can become permanent. Then it happens again. Reality continues because the same moment of destruction is never allowed to complete.

Still happening.

Odin cannot undo the choice without risking the universe he made it to save.

He remembers.

What He Believed He Was Doing

The Overgod's actions become easier to follow once one assumption is accepted: he did not separate creation from responsibility.

He made the world. Therefore he believed he was responsible for what it became. Responsibility, at his scale, did not mean care alone. It meant intervention. If something inside creation threatened the whole, he believed it was his duty to contain it. If containment failed, replace it. If replacement failed, start again.

This is why every rebellion made the next response worse. Resistance did not prove to him that his control was illegitimate. It proved that the problem was deeper than he had estimated.

That logic is internally consistent and still monstrous to anyone forced to live underneath it.

Current Status

The Overgod was severed from this reality. That is the strongest statement the archive can support.

Severed is not the same as destroyed.

There is no reliable record of where an Overgod exists once the reality attached to it has been cut away, whether such a place can meaningfully be called a place, or whether the being remains capable of observing what happens afterward.
